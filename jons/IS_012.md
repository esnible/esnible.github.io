# INDIA: INDIAN NATIVE STATES — TONK STATE

## by Jan Lingen

November 1975

## Geographical Situation

Tonk was an Indian Native State, consisting of six isolated districts partly in Rajastan (Tonk, Aligarh and Nimbhara) and partly in Central India (Chhabra, Pirawa and Sironj ) and separated from each other by distances varying from 20 to 250 miles. The total area of the State was 2,553 square miles and its population in 1901 was 275,201.

## History

In the reign of the Moghul Emperor Mohammed Shah (1131-61 A.H), one Taleh Khan,
a Pathan or Afghan of the Buner tribe, left his home in the Buner country and took service with Ali Mohammed Khan, a Rohilla of distinction. Taleh's son, Haiyat Khan acquired some property in Moradabad, and to him in 1768 Amir Khan, the founder of the State, was born. As a petty mercenary leader, he rose to be commander of a large army in the service of Jaswant Rao Holkar of Indore, He was employed in the campaigns against Sindhia, the Peshwa, and the British,
and in assisting to levy the contributions exacted from Rajputana and Malwa. It was one of the terms of the union between Amir Khan and Holkar that they should share equally all future plunder and conquests, and in 1798 (1212 AH) he received for his services the district of Sironj which had been held by Baji Rao Peshwa from 1736 to 1754 and until 1798 by Jaswant Rao Holkar. In 1806 (1220 AH)
the district of Tonk, after which the State came to be known, was added, Nimbhara following in 1809, and Chhabra in 1816,

On the entrance of the British into Malwar, Amir Khan secured under a treaty of November 1817 (1223 AH), a guaranteed tenure of all the lands he held under grants
from Holkar, on condition that he disbanded his army of fifty-two battalions of disciplined infantry and surrendered his artillery, with the exception of forty guns, as a valuation.

Nawab Amir Khan died in 1834 (1250 AH) and was succeeded by his son, Wazir

Mohammed Khan, who, during the mutiny, repulsed with comparatively few men an attack made on the Tonk fort by the combined forces of the Nawab of Banda and Tantia Topi. For these services, his salute was raised to 17 guns and, in 1862 he received a "sanad " guaranteeing the succession to his family according to Muhammadan law, in the event of the extinction of his line of direct descendents. At his death in 1864 AD (1280 AH) he was succeeded by his son Mohammed Ali Khan, who soon made himself unpopular with his subjects. He forbad the building of Hindu temples, or even repairs to existing ones, and in his overpowering desire
for increased revenue he resorted to every means of wringing money from the jagirdars and cultivators. In consequence of his involvement in a treacherous attack on the Thakur of Lawa, he was deposed on the 8th of August 1867 (1284 AH) and exiled to Benares where he died in 1895 (1313 AH).

Muhammed Ali Khan was succeeded on the 19th January 1868 (1284 AH) by his son Moh-
ammed Ibrahim Ali Khan, but until 1872 (1289 AH) the State was administered by a Council of Regency controlled by a resident British Officer. At the end of the
regency, the capital was moved from Sironj to Tonk city where it remained, Mohammed Ibrahim Ali Khan died on 23rd June, 1930 (1349 AH) and was succeeded by his second son Mohammed Sa'adat Ali Khan who reigned until the State became part of the Indian Republic in 1947.

## Coinage

The main mint until 1873 was Sironj and all coins were struck there. It was known as a mint for copper in the time of Akbar and for silver under the later Moghuls (Farrukhsiyar and later rulers). Prior to 1212 AH, when Sironj came in possession of Amir Khan, silver coins of the following type were struck in the name of Shah Alam II,

Amir Khan may have continued striking this type for the remainder of the reign of Shah Alam (i.e. until 1221 AH), but no specimens are yet known. The first coins which can be attributed to Amir Khan are copper coins, dated 1225 and 1226 AH (Class III A) from Sironj mint, in the name of Muhammed Akbar II. The reverse of these coins bears the design of a horse, referring most probably to the illustrious Pathan horses of Amir Khan's Cavalry.

The earliest date observed on silver coins from Sironj is 1235 AH (Class I A) found on the usual Moghul type in the name of Mohammed Akbar II. This series is known to at least 1269 AH for silver (Class I B) and to 1253 AH for copper

(Class III B). After the Indian Mutiny in 1857 the last Moghul ruler Bahadur Shah was deported to Rangoon and the Indian Native States abandoned the practice
of retaining the Moghul emperor's name on their coinage, Like many other Native States, Tonk acknowledged the British supremacy by introducing a new design of silver coin bearing the name of Queen Victoria on the obverse and the name of the local ruler, Wazir Mohammed Khan, on the reverse (Class I C). The
earliest date known for this type is 1276 AH (= 1859) and they were known as "Muhammed Khani", In 1280 AH a fly-whisk was added to the reverse and they became known as "Chanwar Shahi". This fly-whisk became a prominent mark on all the later coins of Tonk State.

Both silver and copper coins of Sironj mint are known in the name of Queen Victoria and the successor of Wazir Mohammed Khan, Mohammed Ali Khan but all so
far observed are dated after his exile to Benares (Class I E and III E). Possibly the Council of Regency preferred to withhold the right of coining from Mohammed Ibrahim Ali Khan until he came of age and continued to employ the name
of Mohammed Ali Khan. With this series fractions of the rupee denomination are found for the first time struck from the same dies. On many coins of Sironj mint of this period a number can be seen at the left of the mint. Where sufficiently recognisable this seems to be 23 and should be regarded as a fictitious date most probably based on the regnal years of Mohammed Akbar II.

With the end of the regency, Mohammed Ibrahim Ali Khan transferred the capital
and the main mint to Tonk, although Sironj mint continued in operation until at least 1314 AH (1896), striking in his own name copper and silver coins including
fractional rupees (Class I F and IIIF). In place of the fictitious regnal year noted above a Christian date is found on the obverse although only a single copper coin shows a complete date (Class III F Type b).

While the coins struck at Sironj are all very scarce with, in most cases, only
a Single specimen being known, the mint at Tonk provides an almost continuous series from 1290 to 1352 AH apart from the period between 1318 to 1340 AH when
coins are known only for 1329, 1330, 1335 and 1336 AH, crude pisces The earliest copper coins which have been attributed to Tonk mint are/dated 1284
and 1285 AH (1867-68) and these have often been regarded as the earliest coins struck there.

It is interesting to note that as early as 1917, R.G. Gyani attributed these
coins not to Tonk, but to Sidi Ibrahim Khan III (1848-1879) of Janjira (Ref: J.A.S.B. 1934, Numismatic Supplement XLV, page 71-73) and in a letter dated

17th January, 1870 the existence of coining implements at Janjira was reported (Ref: Financial Department Proceedings January 1876, No. 18). As the mint of Janjira was ordered to close by the British in 1834 (Ref: Financial Report No. 720, 19th September, 1834) this attribution was not unanimously accepted.

However, the Financial Department Proceedings also contain a report from Sidi Ibrahim Khan himself saying that the coinage of rupees had been stopped in his territory during the reign of his father Sidi Mohammed Khan (1826-1848) and had never been resumed, but the coinage of copper money had been carried on for some considerable time back, The custom was to give a bar of copper to Dhamoo Kasar, Tambut, who with six others were employed by the Nawab to make the pice. (Ref: Financial Department Proceedings January 1876 No. 136). It seems clear that copper coins were struck at Janjira up to at least 1870 and there is no basis on which to attribute these specimens to Tonk mint. Supporting evidence for this view is provided by a letter of 24th March 1870 from the Assistant Agent, Governor General, Tonk, in which he states "There is only one mint in the principality, and is located at Seronge". (Ref: Financial Department Proceedings January 1876 No. 56.).

The earliest coins struck at Tonk mint are likely to be those dated 1290 AH

(=1873) (Class II F and IV F) and bearing the name of Queen Victoria on the obverse and that of Mohammed Ibrahim Ali Khan on the reverse, Some types of this Class show an AD date on the obverse as well as the normal AH date on the reverse of the coins, This became the general pattern for all later issues from both Tonk and Sironj mints. In 1877 Queen Victoria assumed the title of Empress of India and all issues from this date onwards bear this title (Class II G),

On the copper coins of this period a major part of the legend is normally off the
flan and only the centre portion of the inscription can be observed. This makes it extremely difficult to arrive at a satisfactory reconstruction of the full inscription.

The only known gold coins of Tonk State are of 1 and 2 Mohur denomination and dated 1880/1297 (Class II G - type c). They were struck with dies similar to those used for the silver coins. During the same period some 2 rupee pieces were struck, Two dates 1880AD/1297 AH and 1881AD/1298 AH, are known. No satisfactory explanation has been advanced to explain why the gold coins and the double rupees were struck.

No coins are known yet dated to Edward VII's reign but in 1911/1329 AH a large
number of copper coins were struck (Class IV J - type a to e) 'using at least 5 distinct combinations of dies. A new type of paisa of a better design was introduced in 1917 (Class IV J - f) but for reasons unknown it was produced for a short period only. A crude type of paisa was struck in 1924 and again at regular intervals until 1928. Apart from the rupee of 1912 AD/1330 AH no silver coins are known from the first half of George V's reign, but from 1923 to 1929 most dates are represented and in 1928 4, + and + rupee pieces were produced.

Early in the reign of Mohammed Sa'adat Ali Khan, paises were struck by machine at the Calcutta mint (Class IV - K) the first issue (type a) being dated 1932/1350. These coins being equal in weight and size to the British + anna, were hoarded and soon disappeared from circulation so that in 1934 a second issue had to be struck similar to type a and of the same weight, but smaller in size. This, together with a locally struck 2 anna coin (Class II K), eased the situation for the demand for small change. The 2 anna silver coins, the last issue of Tonk mint, were withdrawn from circulation some five years later, all silver "Chanwar Shahi"
currency being demonetised on the 1st May, 1941. The withdrawal from circulation however, lasted another 12 to 18 months.

The majority of the rupee denominations of Tonk mint are fairly common, although
some types and dates are scarce. The gold coins are extremely rare and the
double rupees can be classed as rare to very rare. As with most other series of Indian Native State coins, the minor denominations are much scarcer than the rupees and this is illustrated by the mintage figures for the year 1926 when 63,923 rupees were struck but minor denominations, (4, + and + rupees) totalled only a value of Rs 2,690.

## Weights

Closely related to the coinage, are some copper tola weights (Class V) dated

1303 and 1308 AH (1886, 1891) which were issued, and perhaps used solely by the Tonk Government. The weight of a tola was 11 mashas and 3 rati (170-625 grains), which is also the weight of the rupee. Tola weights are well known in India, but generally are of a more precious metal like gold, manufactured by Sarafs or bullion merchants and used as stamped bullion. This does not apply to the tola weights of Tonk as they are of a base metal and issued by the Tonk Government, which might indicate that they were intended as standard weights and measures, There may well be other equally valid purposes,

### Note

All silver coins shown on the following plates, if not stated otherwise,
are rupees weighing approximately 170 grains. The weights of the fractional coins
(struck with the same dies) are in proportion to the rupee. The gold coins are
single or double mohurs with 1 mohur = 170.6 grains.

Copper coins of Sironj mint weigh approx. 234 grains. Copper coins of Tonk mint
weigh approx. 123 grains at first but gradually decrease until the latest issues
weigh only 68 grains.
